// about

Hello, my name is Bryant Flores. I am a first generation Mexican-American professional who grew up in East Los Angeles. I graduated in 2021 with a Bachelor of Science in Computer Science with a specialization in Information from the University of California Irvine. I am a father to three cats and their names are Mochi, Luna, and Kiko (short for Federico). During my free time, you can find me hiking the beautiful mountains of California, camping, and spending time looking at the Pacific Ocean on Crystal Cove state beach. On occasion, I go for runs because as a software developer I find the balance between body and mind to be crucial. I have often said to myself, that as a developer and independent thinker, if I couldn't run more than 1 mile than my mind and my thoughts would be useless.

Philosophy is one of the major things in my life that continues to push my mental limits. As a self declared existential theologist, I believe it is absolutely crucial to consider the logical conclusions of our thoughts and beliefs. I strongly believe in a God, and have always considered it a great analogy to the systems we build considering how those systems and programs themselves have creators. It seems absolutely clear to me that we must take our rhetoric and language to build a system that allows for our empathy to follow suit. As an engineer, I consider it my responsibility to have conversations with other fellow professionals about the new and upcoming rise of artificial intelligence.

I would rather not have to imagine Sisyphus happy but instead sweating and smiling, remembering that grace is always within arms reach. I would love to consider us as a species dedicated to sweating and smiling all the more — and in the midst of looking at the mud and darkness, I'd love to light a scented candle.

Repo

Over roughly two years — between 2022 and 2024, while working full-time — I worked through Michael Sugrue and Darren Staloff's "Great Minds of the Western Intellectual Tradition," a lecture series covering 3,000 years of philosophy from ancient Greece through 20th-century critical theory.

I didn't follow a syllabus. I watched lectures and wrote. The philosophy became the interpretive framework through which I read everything else — novels from a book club, theology, the Gospels, Stoic texts. The questions it raised are ones I'm still sitting with.

The Questions That Followed

"Who builds AI systems versus who experiences their consequences?"
"What does technological literacy look like in communities that lack the infrastructure to engage with it on their own terms?"
"How do we talk about AI ethics in communities that lack the technical background to understand what these systems are?"
"What responsibility do engineers have to the people their systems were never designed around?"

These questions sit at the intersection of my engineering background, my philosophical reading, and my own experience as a first-generation Mexican-American professional who understands both how these systems are built and what it feels like to be outside the rooms where they're designed.

Where It Points

I'm pursuing PhD programs in informatics, HCI, and AI ethics. The question I keep returning to is what it means to build systems for people those systems were never designed around. That question requires both technical fluency and the kind of contextual knowledge that comes from being inside communities that are often only studied from the outside.

Recent conversations with faculty have explored LLMs and weapons technology, technological literacy gaps, and the unequal distribution of AI's benefits and harms. The philosophy didn't give me answers. It gave me better questions — and the patience to sit with them.

In 2024, I served as a judge for the UCI Donald Bren School of ICS Senior Capstone Expo — evaluating student projects across software engineering and human-computer interaction. It was a reminder that the best technical work always has a person on the other end of it.
